Judge brief — PayPal AI Hackathon 2026

Every payment asks you to work something out. This product works it out first.

A shopper is asked to agree to an amount they cannot check: what it totals, what leaving costs, what leaves their account today. The companion states all three before the agreement, from the seller's own terms and PayPal's own objects — never from an estimate.

01

What this does

It reads a commitment — a purchase, a subscription, a bill, or a document someone was sent — and shows three figures in the order the decision needs them, with the arithmetic that produced each one:

FigureWhy it is firstExample
Across the termThe number nobody can compute from a page that advertises $12 a bag.$144.00
Cost to leaveWhat the agreement costs to undo, or that the seller never said.$0.00
Charged todayThe one figure a merchant already makes unmissable.$12.00

02

Where the numbers come from

  • PayPal supplies the money: Orders v2 for a purchase, Subscriptions and Billing Plans for a repeating one, Invoicing v2 for a bill. The amount and its breakdown are read back from PayPal, not restated by the page.
  • The seller's published terms supply the rest: term length, instalments, fees. The arithmetic is ordinary code with tests, not a guess by a model.
  • For a pasted document, a language model is asked for the sentences that state the terms and nothing else. Every sentence it returns is checked as a literal substring of the text. A figure whose sentence cannot be found is discarded, and the screen says how many were discarded.
  • A guard drops any figure the code did not derive. The model never produces a number; it produces a quotation.

03

What it will not do

  • No advice. No verdict, no score, no “you should”. It states the commitment and stops.
  • No estimate. A term the seller never published is shown as Not stated, with what that means. It is never filled in.
  • No profile. Nothing is inferred about the reader. The only adjustment is the reader's own: text size, one figure at a time, read-aloud.
  • No storage. Nothing about a person is written down. The record after payment is the only copy, and it lives in the shopper's hands.
  • No client-side money. PayPal objects are created on the server, when the shopper acts — never when a page renders.

04

What is not real

  • Payments run in PayPal's sandbox. No real money moves.
  • The catalogue is an example. Three products and two bills, written to contain the ambiguity the product exists to remove — a discount price, a monthly term, an instalment plan, a biller who leaves out what happens after a missed payment.
  • No user testing. The design rests on published research into cognitive accessibility and checkout behaviour, not on participants. The claim is that the commitment is legible, not that outcomes improved.
  • The model's wording is not guaranteed. If it is unavailable, the figures remain — computed from PayPal — and the screen says the plain-language summary is missing.

How to check it

The repository's README carries the setup and the sandbox credentials. The fastest honest check is the document path: paste any payment terms and compare each figure to the sentence printed underneath it.

Screenshots and the demo video live in the submission itself. Nothing on this page is a mockup; every description above names behaviour the code performs.
