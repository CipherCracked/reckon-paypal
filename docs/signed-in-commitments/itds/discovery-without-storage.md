# ITD 23 — Finding a Person's Records Without Storing Them

| ITD 23 — *"Use the merchant's subscription list, read one at a time, to find a payer's records."* |  |
| :---- | :---- |
| **THE PROBLEM** | ITD 22 produces a payer id. PayPal offers no endpoint that takes one and returns what that payer agreed to: Order v2 has seven endpoints and none of them lists (reference, checked 2026-11-11), and `GET /v1/billing/subscriptions` is *"list all subscriptions for merchant account"* — filtered by `plan_ids`, `statuses` and dates, and returning only `{id, status}` (called live: two subscriptions, both `CANCELLED`). The payer is only on the per-id read, `subscriber.payer_id`. So the only route from a signed-in person to their commitments runs through everything this merchant holds, read until it matches — or through something we store when the subscription is created. |
| **OPTIONS CONSIDERED (Decision in bold)** | 1. **Enumerate the merchant's subscriptions, read each id, keep the ones whose `subscriber.payer_id` equals the signed-in payer id — bounded to `MAX_PAGES = 5` (100 subscriptions), `READ_CONCURRENCY = 5`, and a total deadline; on a bound being hit, return what was found plus a flag saying the scan stopped.** <br> 2. Write a `payer_id → [subscription ids]` mapping into a store when a subscription is created or approved, and read it back on demand. <br> 3. Put the approved subscription id in the person's signed cookie at the moment of approval, so the page reads its own memory rather than PayPal's. <br> 4. Ask the person to paste the subscription id they were given. |
| **REASONING** | Options 2 and 3 are the same trade dressed differently: they buy speed and a complete history by keeping a record of what a person bought. That record is exactly what the product tells people it does not keep, and it is the record that becomes a liability the moment the app is deployed — a list of people and their recurring charges, on a server, for a demo with no operator, no retention policy and no way to delete it. Option 3 is worse than option 2 in one respect: it puts the list on the person's device in a form that authorises nothing and expires with the cookie, so it is a stale copy that cannot be refreshed and disappears the moment they sign out. <br><br> Option 4 is what the product already does — it hands over a receipt link — and the entire reason for this work is that people lose it. <br><br> Option 1 keeps the product's promise literally: nothing is written, every visit asks PayPal again, and the answer is as current as the moment it was asked. Its cost is a scan whose length is set by the merchant's history rather than by the person, which is a real limit and the reason the bound must be *visible*. A scan that silently stops at a hundred subscriptions and presents itself as complete is the same failure as an estimate; the page says it stopped (`page.stopped`) and IPD 26 gives it the "could not check" wording. <br><br> Matching is on `payer_id`, not email. An email is not an identity — two sandbox accounts can share one, and a person can change theirs — while `payer_id` is the account number PayPal uses to route the money. The verified email is kept in the cookie for display only. <br><br> The fan-out is deliberately serialised in **bounded concurrent batches** rather than fired all at once: sandbox rate limits are unforgiving and a page that 429s is a page that says "could not check" for a reason of our own making. |
| **TRADEOFFS** | The page's cost grows with this merchant's total subscriptions, not with the person's. At the hackathon's scale (two records) that is three calls and about a second; for a merchant with thousands it is not viable, and the honest fix is an index PayPal does not offer — which is why this is the demo's answer and not the product's forever answer. <br><br> A cancelled subscription still costs a read to discover, so a person with many of them pays for pages that will not match. <br><br> One-time purchases and bills are invisible: Orders v2 cannot be listed and invoices are merchant-scoped by date, so the page states that it shows subscriptions rather than leaving the absence to be read as "you bought nothing else". |
| **NOTES** | If the scan hits its bound, `GET /api/account/subscriptions` returns `{ subscriptions, stopped: true }` and the page says it stopped. The deadline is shared with the page (the same `withBudget` the reader uses), so a slow PayPal produces "could not check" rather than a spinner that outlives the request. The fan-out is one module (`lib/paypal/records.ts`) so a future index replaces one function rather than the page. |
