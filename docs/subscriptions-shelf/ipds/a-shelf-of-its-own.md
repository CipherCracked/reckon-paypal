# IPD 42 — A Shelf of Its Own

**Status:** Decided (2026-11-12)
**Date:** 2026-11-12
**Answers:** Problem tree Q3 of `../intro.md` — *where does the shelf live, and how does a person reach it?*
**Serves:** IPD 41 (the shelf of words); IPD 33 and IPD 34, whose shop opens on departments and carries the search beneath them.

---

## Problem to solve

The shelf has to be somewhere, and the shop's opening screen is already spoken for. IPD 33 chose it deliberately: the sixteen departments come first because a visitor who cannot name a product should meet recognisable signs rather than a question, and the search sits beneath as the shortcut. A second grid bolted under the departments asks that visitor to decide what kind of page they are on, and makes every shop visit pay for a search nobody browsing a department asked for.

It also has to be reachable from where the worry is named. The front door opens on a worked listing and says what the product does; a person who reads it and thinks *I have one of those* should not have to guess which department a subscription lives in.

## Options

### Option 1 — **A screen of its own, linked from the shop and from the front door**

`/shop/subscriptions` is one grid, one heading and one sentence saying what the shelf is: each tile is a listing whose own words say it charges again, and the phrase that says so is quoted from the listing. The shop's department shelf gains one line beneath it — a link, not a section. The front door gains the same link where it names the worry.

*How it feels:* a person reading the front door follows one link and lands on a shelf of things that repeat, each captioned in the seller's words. A person browsing departments sees the same link where they are already looking for a way in.

### Option 2 — A section beneath the department grid on the shop's opening screen

The shop root shows departments, then "What charges more than once" with its tiles.

*How it feels:* the shop root becomes two shelves and two kinds of navigation. A person scrolling past the departments meets a second question — *did I want departments or repeats?* — on the screen whose whole job was to ask nothing. The shop root also pays for two catalogue searches on every visit, including the visits that only browse a department.

### Option 3 — Replace the department shelf with the shelf of repeats

The shop opens on subscriptions, with the departments beneath.

*How it feels:* a shop for one kind of thing — and not even a true one, since the shelf's coverage is a sample of the catalogue. A person looking for a toaster, or holding a link to one, opens a page that says *subscriptions*. It also reverses IPD 33, whose reasoning — the catalogue's own structure is the shop's structure — the shelf does not overturn.

## Reasoning

The deciding factor is **what each screen asks before it shows anything**. IPD 33 chose the shop's opening screen to ask nothing; a shelf of repeats is a question with a point of view (*are you here for something that charges again?*), and two questions on one screen is the thing the shop was fixed for. A screen of its own keeps each screen's ask singular: the shop asks *what department?*, the shelf asks *which of these?*, the product page asks *do you understand this commitment?*

The second factor is **the front door is where the worry arrives**. The shelf is the answer to a sentence the front door already says, so the link belongs beside that sentence. A section on the shop root would be reachable only by a person who had already gone to the shop for another reason.

The third factor is **cost on the pages that were not asked for it**. The shop root is opened many times to browse; its two catalogue calls are already spent on the departments and the products. Putting two more searches on it charges every one of those visits for the shelf. On its own route, the shelf's two searches are paid only by the people who came for the shelf — and a page's cache entry is the link that produced it, which on a route of its own is exactly one page.

Option 2 is rejected because it puts two asks and four catalogue calls on one screen; Option 3 because the shelf cannot carry the shop, and because it silently reverses a decision for a reason that is about coverage, not structure.

## Tradeoffs

- **One more route, one more link, one more screen to keep honest.** A person who never follows a link never learns the shelf exists, and the shop's opening screen — the screen a stranger judges — says nothing about the product's subject.
- **The shelf inherits the shop's failure sentences** rather than having its own, which is right for the catalogue's key and wrong for its own emptiness: an empty shelf and an unreachable catalogue are different sentences on a screen that has nothing else to show.
- **Coverage is a sample, and the screen must own it.** A shelf on its own route reads as *the catalogue's subscriptions*; a line beneath the departments would have read as a rail. The sentence above the grid carries more weight here.

## Notes

- The link's words are the shelf's only advertisement: something a person can recognise without being told what a "commitment" is.
- The shop's opening screen keeps its own ordering untouched — departments, then the search, then the pasted link — which is what IPD 33 built and IPD 36 reordered.
- The front door's link is one line, not a second call to action beside its worked example.
